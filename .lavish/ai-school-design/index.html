Q1

What is the app actually made of

queued

The decision. A "macOS app" can be built two ways, and this choice is the hardest one to undo later. Everything else in the plan hangs off it.

The gloss. SwiftUI is Apple's own toolkit. You write Swift, and you get a genuinely native Mac app: fast, small, feels exactly like Mail or Notes. Tauri wraps a web page (HTML, React) in a real Mac window. You get a proper .app in your Applications folder, but the inside is a web page. Electron is the same idea, heavier, and it is what VS Code and Slack are.

What changes downstream. Your lessons need to render interactive things: a slider that moves a curve, a maths formula, a small code editor, a chart. On the web those are solved and free. In SwiftUI you would build each one by hand, or embed a web view anyway and end up hybrid. The other side: with SwiftUI an agent writing your code has far less training data to draw on, and Swift UI bugs are harder for you to diagnose yourself.

A worked example. Say the constructor writes a lesson on gradient descent with a widget where you drag the learning rate and watch the path bounce. In React that is roughly forty lines using a charting library. In SwiftUI it is a custom Canvas view, hit testing, and your own animation, and it is not reusable for the next widget.

Recommendation: Tauri + React + TypeScript. The reason is not taste, it is that the content this app displays is generated at runtime by a model. A model can reliably emit HTML and a fixed set of interactive block types. It cannot emit SwiftUI views that compile. That single fact decides it. SwiftUI wins instead if you want this in the menu bar, in Spotlight, in Shortcuts, and on an iPad later. Say so and I will flip.
Q2

How the app reaches a model

queued

The decision. You said "constructor harness plus model". A harness is the program that drives the model: it holds the conversation, gives the model tools, and loops until the job is done. Claude Code is a harness. The raw API is not.

Why it matters here. Building a good course needs the model to search the web, read pages, write many files, and check its own work. That is agentic behaviour, and it needs a harness. Grading one multiple choice answer needs none of that: one question in, one verdict out. Using a full agent for that is slow and wasteful.

What changes downstream. If the app shells out to Claude Code, it inherits your logins, your skills, and web search for free, but the app now depends on a CLI being installed and it is harder to show progress in a nice UI. If the app calls the API directly, it is self-contained and the UI is smooth, but you must build search and file tools yourself.

A worked example. You type "Bayesian statistics, I know calculus, 6 weeks, I want to be able to read a paper". Option A spawns claude -p in the background; it searches, finds the good lecture notes, writes twelve lesson files and sixty tasks over four minutes, and the app streams its progress. Option B sends one big prompt and hopes the model knows good resources from memory.

Recommendation: split it. The two jobs have opposite shapes. Construction is rare, slow, expensive, and needs tools. Grading is frequent, must feel instant, and needs none. Forcing them down one path makes one of them bad. Your Settings screen then honestly offers two independent choices, which is what you asked for. Direct-API-only wins instead if you ever want to hand this app to someone who does not have Claude Code installed.
Q3

Is a course a finished thing, or a living one

queued

The decision. After the constructor finishes, does the course stay exactly as written until you ask for changes, or does the app quietly rewrite parts of it as it learns where you struggle?

The gloss. Khan Academy is mostly the first: a fixed tree of content, and the adaptivity is in which bits it shows you. A "living" course goes further and generates new content on the fly, for example three extra practice problems on the exact thing you keep failing.

What changes downstream. Fixed means you can read the whole course up front, trust it, and print it. Living means the course you finish is not the course you started, you cannot easily review what changed, and every generation costs money. But living is far better at fixing a genuine gap.

The middle option. The course is fixed, but the app can append. When you fail the same objective three times, it offers to generate a remediation block, and that block is added to the course folder as a new, clearly labelled file. Nothing already written is ever silently edited.

Recommendation: fixed with opt-in remediation. A course you cannot trust to stay put is hard to plan around, and silent rewrites make it impossible to tell whether you improved or the questions got easier. Append-only keeps that honesty and still gives you the rescue path when you are genuinely stuck. Fully living wins instead if you expect to abandon courses halfway and want the app to fight for your attention. I do not think that is your problem.
Q4

What "ramping up" actually means mechanically

queued

The decision. You want tasks to get harder as you advance. There are three different machines that produce that feeling, and they cost wildly different amounts to build.

The gloss. Gating means tier 3 stays locked until you pass tier 2. It is about twenty lines of code. A mastery model means the app keeps a number per objective, raises it when you succeed, decays it over time, and picks your next task from whichever objective is weakest. That is the Khan Academy engine, and it is a real piece of software.

What changes downstream. Gating is a straight line: you climb the ladder module by module. A mastery model can send you back down to a tier 1 card on a topic from three weeks ago because it thinks you have forgotten it. That is more effective and it is also more machinery, more places to be wrong, and it needs many more questions per objective to draw from.

The thing worth noting. You can build gating now and add the mastery model later without throwing anything away, but only if the app records every single attempt with its objective and outcome from day one. That recording costs almost nothing today and is impossible to reconstruct later.

Recommendation: gated ladder plus review queue. A mastery model with too few questions per objective just shows you the same three cards on a timer, which feels broken and teaches you the cards rather than the idea. Gating gives you the ramp you asked for immediately. The review queue gives you most of the retention benefit for a fraction of the work. Recording every attempt keeps the upgrade path open. The full model wins instead if the constructor reliably produces thirty-plus items per objective. We will know that after the first real course, and we can revisit it then.
Q5

What happens when the grader says you failed

queued

The decision. You upload a week of work. A model reads it and is not impressed. What does the app do, and can you argue back?

Why this is not a small question. This is the single most likely thing to make you stop using the app. A grader that is harsh, vague, or wrong on a piece of work you spent six hours on is worse than no grader at all. It is also the one place where the app has power over you, so it needs a way to be overruled.

The gloss. A rubric is the list of criteria the grader scores against, written when the task was created. Showing it to you before you start turns grading from a verdict into a checklist. Evidence quotes mean the grader must quote the part of your submission that earned each score, which makes a bad grade visibly bad rather than merely annoying.

What changes downstream. A blocking grader needs to be right, so it needs a second opinion pass and an appeal flow. A non-blocking grader is advice, so it can be wrong occasionally without damage. That decides how much machinery goes around it.

Recommendation: advisory, rubric upfront, disputable. You are the only user and there is no certificate at the end, so a hard block buys you nothing and can cost you the habit. The rubric being visible in advance is where most of the value sits anyway: it tells you what good looks like before you start, which is most of what a good course does. Blocking wins instead if you know from experience that you skip ahead and leave things half-learned. You know that about yourself better than I do.
