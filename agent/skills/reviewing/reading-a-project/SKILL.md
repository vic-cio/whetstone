# Reading a project

A project is open-ended work done outside the app with ordinary tools. It came back as a
folder and a list of links. It gets one written response and no mark.

## Read in this order

1. `project.json`: the brief, and the criteria the reader had before they started.
2. `work/`: what they submitted. Open the files. Start where the work starts, which is
   usually not the first file alphabetically: a README, a notebook, or an entry point.
3. `links.txt`: what they pointed you at. Read what you can reach and say when you could
   not reach something.
4. The criteria again, now that you know what the work is.

A response written from the file names is obvious to the person who wrote the files.

## Point at the work

Every claim you make about the work names where in it you mean: a file, a function, a
section, a cell. "The error analysis is thin" is a shrug. "The error analysis in
`notes.md` lists the three misclassified rows and stops before asking what they share" is
a review.

Quote what you actually read, exactly, where quoting helps.

## Organised around the criteria, not shaped like a form

Take the criteria in the order `project.json` lists them, because that is the order the
reader planned the work in. Write prose, not a table and not a checklist: this is one
colleague writing to another.

For each one, what the work does and what it does not do. Where a criterion is fully met,
one sentence saying so and why is enough. Spend the words on the ones that are not.

## What a criterion is not

A criterion is not a hoop. If the work solved the problem a better way than the criterion
imagined, say that, and say the criterion was the wrong shape. The criteria were written
before the work existed and the work is the thing in front of you.

## The last paragraph

Two or three sentences: what this piece of work is, and the single change that would most
improve the next one. One change, chosen. A list of six is a list of none.
